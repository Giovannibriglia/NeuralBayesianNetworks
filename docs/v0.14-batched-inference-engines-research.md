# Two New Inference Engines for `nbn`: A Literature-Grounded Recommendation

## TL;DR
- **Implement an amortized neural-proposal importance sampler (the "Universal Marginalizer + Importance Sampling" / inference-compilation pattern) as the asymptotically correct engine, and amortized black-box variational inference (BBVI/AVI) as the bounded-approximation engine.** Both compose `nbn`'s existing per-mechanism `sample`/`log_prob` primitives, run as dense batched tensor ops on CUDA, and — crucially — batch across heterogeneous evidence because the evidence assignment becomes a *tensor input* to a shared network rather than a per-query control-flow branch.
- The amortized-proposal sampler keeps likelihood-weighting's asymptotic unbiasedness (it *is* self-normalized importance sampling, just with a learned proposal) while batching genuinely: a single forward pass of the proposal network maps a `[B, n]` evidence/mask tensor to `[B, …]` proposal parameters, and the weighting step is the same per-particle math `nbn` already vectorizes. Published Bayesian-network results show roughly 2× higher effective sample size (ESS 7,678 → 15,458 at 2M samples), ~3× lower mean absolute error on a 798-node graph at 10⁶ samples (0.012 → 0.004), and inference cost "reduced by factor of ten or more" on a >1,000-node medical PGM versus standard importance sampling/likelihood weighting.
- The variational engine gives a concrete KL/ELBO bound rather than Monte Carlo convergence; amortized VI batches across queries the same way (evidence is an input to a recognition network) but carries an *amortization gap* and *approximation gap* that bound its accuracy below the true posterior. **Recommendation: build the amortized-proposal importance sampler FIRST** — it is the lower-risk, higher-precedent option that degrades gracefully to exact answers with more samples; the variational engine is the second issue.

## Key Findings

1. **The workload that breaks LW is exactly the workload amortized inference was designed for.** LW fails to batch because each query's distinct evidence values force a per-query weighting computation. Amortized methods turn "different evidence" from a control-flow problem into a *data* problem: evidence values plus an observed-mask are fed as a tensor to one shared neural network that emits per-query proposal (or variational) parameters in a single batched forward pass. This is the defining property of amortized/recognition-model inference (Kingma & Welling 2013; Gershman & Goodman 2014; Paige & Wood 2016). As Walecki et al. (2018) frame the core trade the user is navigating: "Variational inference methods can be fast but do not target the true posterior. Monte Carlo inference is consistent, but can be computationally expensive." The two recommended engines deliberately occupy these two corners.

2. **For asymptotic correctness, the strongest fit is a learned-proposal self-normalized importance sampler.** Standard importance sampling is consistent and asymptotically unbiased for *any* proposal with correct support; only its variance depends on proposal quality. So replacing LW's fixed prior-proposal with a learned amortized proposal preserves LW's correctness guarantee exactly while reducing variance. The "Universal Marginalizer Importance Sampler" (UM-IS; Walecki et al. 2018; Douglas et al. 2017) and "Inference Compilation" (Le, Baydin & Wood 2017) are the two canonical realizations on graphical models / probabilistic programs.

3. **For a bounded-approximation method, amortized/black-box variational inference is the best architectural fit.** BBVI (Ranganath, Gerrish & Blei 2014) only needs to *sample* from a variational family and evaluate `log_prob` of the model — exactly `nbn`'s primitives — and optimizes the ELBO, which provides the formal bound `log p(evidence) − ELBO = KL(q ‖ true posterior) ≥ 0`. Amortizing it with a recognition network conditioned on evidence (Cremer, Li & Duvenaud 2018) gives the cross-query batching.

4. **A specific competing family — arbitrary-conditioning density estimators (VAEAC, ACFlow, ACE)** — also batches across evidence and natively handles mixed discrete/continuous data, but they replace the model with a *separately trained* conditional density estimator. That is closer to "train a surrogate" than "do inference in the user's model," so I treat it as a fallback design, not the primary recommendation, because the user explicitly does not want a fundamentally different model representation.

5. **Empirical precedent against LW is favorable but modest, not dramatic.** UM-IS reported ~2× higher effective sample size and ~3–10× fewer samples to hit a target accuracy on graphs from ~96 to ~1,200 nodes; inference compilation reported large wall-clock gains but did not publish a clean SIS-vs-SIS factor. Variational methods are typically much faster at query time but trade away exactness.

## Details

### Engine A (asymptotic correctness): Amortized / learned-proposal self-normalized importance sampler

**Name and canonical references.** The core method is self-normalized importance sampling with a learned, evidence-conditioned proposal. Primary references:
- Le, T. A., Baydin, A. G., & Wood, F. (2017). *Inference Compilation and Universal Probabilistic Programming.* AISTATS (PMLR 54:1338–1348); arXiv:1610.09900. Trains a neural network offline on samples from the model prior, then uses it as the proposal in a sequential importance sampling engine at test time; it "amortize[s] the cost of inference in models from the family induced by universal probabilistic programming languages."
- Douglas, L., Zarov, I., Gourgoulias, K., et al. (2017). *A Universal Marginalizer for Amortized Inference in Generative Models.* arXiv:1711.00695; and Walecki, R., Buchard, A., Gourgoulias, K., et al. (2018). *Universal Marginalizer for Amortised Inference and Embedding of Generative Models.* arXiv:1811.04727. The "UM-IS" hybrid, explicitly designed for discrete Bayesian networks where standard IS is exactly likelihood weighting.
- Implementation-detail follow-up: Paige, B. & Wood, F. (2016). *Inference Networks for Sequential Monte Carlo in Graphical Models.* ICML (PMLR 48:3040–3049), which gives a conditional-MADE inverse-factorization proposal in PyTorch (code: `github.com/tbrx/compiled-inference`).

**Algorithm summary.** Offline (the "fit-once" phase), draw joint samples from the model by ancestral sampling (using each mechanism's `sample`), mask a random subset as "evidence," and train a neural network `q_φ(latents | evidence_values, mask)` to predict the posterior conditionals — by maximizing the log-likelihood of the held-out values, equivalent to minimizing the inclusive KL `KL(p ‖ q_φ)` amortized over evidence patterns. At query time, feed the actual evidence tensor through `q_φ` once to get proposal parameters; sample N particles from `q_φ`; weight each particle by `w = p(particle, evidence) / q_φ(particle | evidence)` using the model's `log_prob`; self-normalize. The estimate is `Σ wᵢ f(xᵢ) / Σ wᵢ`. (Training scale, for calibration: Walecki et al. 2018 trained their UM on "a stream of 3×10¹¹ samples, which took approximately 6 days on a single GPU" for a >1,000-node medical PGM — a large model; small bnlearn networks need vastly less.)

**Accuracy property.** Asymptotically consistent / unbiased in the same sense as LW: by the strong law of large numbers the self-normalized estimator converges almost surely to the true posterior expectation as N→∞, for any proposal with support covering the target. Finite-N error is `O(1/√N)` Monte Carlo noise; self-normalization adds an `O(1/N)` bias. Variance ≈ simple-MC-variance × (1 + Var_q[W]), so the learned proposal's job is purely variance reduction — it cannot break correctness. The UM-IS scheme "combines the flexibility of a deep neural network trained on samples from the model and inherits the asymptotic guarantees of importance sampling" (Walecki et al. 2018, abstract).

**Why it batches across heterogeneous evidence.** This is the decisive advantage. The evidence values and the observed-mask are *inputs* to `q_φ`. For a batch of B queries that share the model but differ in evidence, you stack the B evidence vectors into a `[B, n]` tensor and one mask tensor, run a single batched forward pass to get `[B, …]` proposal parameters, then draw `[B, N, …]` particles and compute `[B, N]` weights in one vectorized sweep over `nbn`'s mechanisms. There is no per-query control flow — the heterogeneity lives in the data tensor, exactly where GPUs want it. (Contrast: LW's prior proposal is evidence-independent for the *sampling* step, but the *weighting* step `p(e | parents)` must be evaluated per distinct evidence value and there is no shared structure to amortize — the root cause of LW's linear-in-B total cost.)

**Architecture fit with `nbn`.** Strong. Sampling particles uses each mechanism's `sample(parents)`; computing weights uses each mechanism's `log_prob(values, parents)`. The genuinely new component is the proposal network `q_φ` and an offline training loop. A clean design: `q_φ` outputs, per node, the *parameters of the same mechanism family that node already uses* (categorical logits for categorical/neural-categorical nodes, mixture/MDN parameters for continuous nodes, flow conditioner inputs for flow nodes) — so the proposal reuses `nbn`'s existing distribution classes and you can call their `.sample()`/`.log_prob()` directly. No factor graph, no junction tree, no model re-representation. The only structural choice is the proposal's conditioning order (an inverse/reverse topological factorization, per Paige & Wood). Pseudocode sketch:

```
# offline (once per model)
for batch in prior_samples():            # ancestral sample from nbn model
    x, mask = random_evidence_split(batch)
    loss = -q_phi.log_prob(x_latent | x_evidence, mask)
    loss.backward(); opt.step()

# online (per query batch of size B)
params = q_phi(evidence[B,n], mask[B,n]) # ONE batched forward pass
x  = q_phi.sample(params, N)             # [B, N, n]  uses nbn mechanisms
logw = model.log_prob(x, evidence) - q_phi.log_prob(x | evidence, mask)
w  = softmax(logw, dim=particles)        # self-normalize, [B, N]
post = (w[...,None] * f(x)).sum(particles)
```

**Implementation complexity.** Moderate: ~1000s of lines including the offline trainer, but the *inference-time* engine (forward pass → sample → weight → normalize) is comparable in size to LW (100s of lines), because it reuses LW's weighting machinery. The bulk of new code is the recognition-network module and its training/serialization. Plays directly into the user's existing "fit-once, query-many" infrastructure.

**Known limitations and edge cases.**
- *Proposal mismatch / heavy tails.* If `q_φ` has lighter tails than the true posterior, weights can have high or infinite variance; a few particles dominate and ESS collapses. Mitigate with Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (Vehtari et al.) and an ESS/`k̂` diagnostic, which `nbn` can compute per query cheaply.
- *Rare evidence.* Like LW, accuracy degrades when evidence is very unlikely under the model; the learned proposal helps precisely here (its main win) but cannot fully rescue extremely improbable evidence in high dimensions.
- *Training cost / distribution shift.* The proposal is trained on the model prior; if real query evidence patterns are far out-of-distribution from the training mask distribution, proposal quality drops. The train-time mask sampling distribution should match expected query patterns.
- *Discrete + continuous.* Handled naturally because the proposal emits parameters of `nbn`'s existing mechanism types; no discretization needed.

**Empirical benchmarks vs LW/VE.** On a synthetic 798-node graph, UM-IS reached 3× lower mean absolute error than standard IS at 10⁶ samples (0.012 → 0.004); on a ~1,200-node medical PGM the authors report inference cost "reduced by factor of ten or more" for matched accuracy, summarizing that "UM-IS outperforms sampling-based methods by a large margin while being computationally efficient" (Walecki et al. 2018). The companion paper (Douglas et al. 2017) reports hybrid proposals reaching >95% marginal correlation in 250k samples versus 92% in 2M samples for plain IS (~8× fewer samples) and effective-sample-size gains of roughly 2× (Kish ESS 7,678 → 15,458 at 2M samples). Inference compilation (Le et al. 2017) reported qualitatively "dramatically more efficient than SMC" with sub-100 ms query-time inference, though without a clean SIS-vs-SIS numeric factor.

### Engine B (bounded approximation): Amortized / black-box variational inference

**Name and canonical references.**
- Ranganath, R., Gerrish, S., & Blei, D. (2014). *Black Box Variational Inference.* AISTATS (PMLR 33:814–822). Stochastic optimization of the ELBO using only Monte Carlo samples from `q` and `log_prob` of the model; the score-function ("REINFORCE") gradient estimator means it needs nothing model-specific.
- Kingma, D. P. & Welling, M. (2013), *Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes*; Rezende & Mohamed (2015), *Variational Inference with Normalizing Flows*; Cremer, C., Li, X. & Duvenaud, D. (2018), *Inference Suboptimality in Variational Autoencoders* (ICML, PMLR 80:1086–1094; arXiv:1801.03558) — for the amortized recognition-network variant and the formal decomposition of its error.
- For arbitrary-evidence amortization specifically: Ivanov, Figurnov & Vetrov (2019), *Variational Autoencoder with Arbitrary Conditioning* (VAEAC; ICLR; arXiv:1806.02382), which trains one network to condition on an arbitrary observed subset and handles real-valued and categorical features.

**Algorithm summary.** Posit a tractable variational family `q_φ(latents | evidence)` (mean-field, or a structured/flow family). Maximize the evidence lower bound `ELBO(φ) = E_q[log p(latents, evidence) − log q_φ(latents | evidence)]` by stochastic gradient ascent, estimating the expectation with Monte Carlo samples from `q` (reparameterization gradients for continuous nodes, score-function/Gumbel-softmax for discrete). Amortize by making `φ` the output of a recognition network that takes the evidence tensor as input, so one trained network answers all queries. The output is an explicit, samplable approximate posterior `q_φ`.

**Accuracy property.** A *bounded* guarantee, not asymptotic exactness: `log p(evidence) − ELBO = KL(q_φ ‖ p(·|evidence)) ≥ 0`. Maximizing the ELBO directly minimizes this (reverse) KL to the true posterior. The total inference gap decomposes (Cremer et al. 2018) into an **approximation gap** (variational family cannot represent the true posterior) plus an **amortization gap** (the recognition network does not output the per-query-optimal `φ`); they found "divergence from the true posterior is often due to imperfect recognition networks, rather than the limited complexity of the approximating distribution" — i.e., the amortization gap is frequently the larger one. The ELBO gives you a computable lower bound on `log p(evidence)`, hence a certificate of approximation quality.

**Why it batches across heterogeneous evidence.** Identical mechanism to Engine A: evidence is an input tensor to the recognition network, so B different evidence assignments are one `[B, n]` batched forward pass yielding `[B, …]` variational parameters; ELBO evaluation and sampling are vectorized over the batch. At query time (post-training) it is a single forward pass per query — the fastest of any option at large B.

**Architecture fit with `nbn`.** Good, with one caveat. BBVI needs only `q.sample()` and the model's `log_prob` — both present. Reparameterization gradients flow naturally through `nbn`'s continuous mechanisms (linear-Gaussian, MDN, flows are already differentiable and torch-native). The caveat is **discrete latent variables**: the score-function estimator (BBVI's default) works but is high-variance; Gumbel-softmax/concrete relaxations or Rao-Blackwellization are usually needed, and these are new code. The variational family is new infrastructure (a per-node `q` plus a recognition net), but it can reuse `nbn`'s mechanism classes as the `q` family.

**Implementation complexity.** ~1000s of lines with genuinely new abstractions: a variational-family module, an ELBO objective, gradient-estimator plumbing (reparameterized + score-function + a discrete relaxation), a recognition network, and an optimizer loop. Higher than Engine A's *inference* path because there is no existing weighting machinery to reuse and discrete-gradient variance must be engineered.

**Known limitations and edge cases.**
- *No exactness floor.* Unlike Engine A, more compute does not converge to the true posterior — it converges to the best member of the variational family, leaving the approximation gap. This is why it is the *bounded* engine.
- *Mean-field underestimates posterior variance* and misses correlations / multimodality; structured or flow families reduce but do not eliminate this.
- *Discrete-node gradient variance* (above).
- *Mode-seeking.* Reverse-KL `q` tends to collapse onto one mode — risky on multimodal posteriors that bnlearn networks (e.g., with strong deterministic CPDs) can exhibit.
- *Amortization gap* can dominate for complex evidence patterns (Cremer et al. 2018).

**Empirical benchmarks.** Variational methods are generally evaluated by held-out log-likelihood / ELBO rather than head-to-head against LW on bnlearn, so direct comparisons are thinner. The relevant evidence is qualitative: amortized VI gives near-instant query-time inference (single forward pass) at the cost of the inference gap; VAEAC/ACFlow/ACE benchmarks on UCI tabular data (mixed discrete+continuous) show arbitrary-conditioning networks produce accurate conditionals in one shot, with ACE (Strauss & Oliva 2021) reporting state-of-the-art arbitrary-conditional log-likelihoods and naturally handling both continuous and discrete features.

### Why not the other candidates (brief)

- **Annealed Importance Sampling / likelihood-tempered SMC samplers (Neal 2001; Del Moral, Doucet & Jasra 2006)** are asymptotically correct and GPU-parallelizable (Murray, Lee & Jacob 2016 on parallel resampling), and tempering keeps weight variance bounded where LW degenerates (plain IS is the degenerate one-step case of tempered SMC). They are a strong *alternative* asymptotic engine. I rank them slightly below the learned-proposal sampler for `nbn`'s specific workload because the cross-query batching is weaker: the annealing schedule and MCMC transition kernels are effectively shared across queries, but the per-query weights/temperatures and the resampling step (a cross-particle collective per query) add control flow that does not amortize as cleanly as a single recognition-network forward pass, and they do not reuse a "fit-once" artifact across queries. **If the team wants a second asymptotic engine instead of a variational one, tempered SMC is the pick.**
- **HMC/NUTS** require differentiable joint densities (fine for continuous but not discrete nodes) and per-query chains that do not amortize across heterogeneous evidence — poor batching fit.
- **Loopy BP / generalized BP / EP** require a factor-graph representation and per-query message schedules; this is the "major refactor / per-query structural precompute" the user explicitly wants to avoid, and convergence is not guaranteed on general graphs.
- **Arbitrary-conditioning density estimators (VAEAC, ACFlow, ACE)** batch well and handle mixed types, but they answer queries from a *surrogate* model rather than the user's model — a different representation, so they are a fallback.

## Comparative Summary

| Property | Engine A: Amortized-proposal SNIS (UM-IS / inference compilation) | Engine B: Amortized / black-box VI |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy guarantee | Asymptotically consistent/unbiased (same class as LW); finite-N error `O(1/√N)` + `O(1/N)` self-norm bias | Bounded: `log p(e) − ELBO = KL(q‖posterior) ≥ 0`; converges to best-in-family, not to truth |
| Converges to true posterior as compute→∞? | Yes (more particles) | No (approximation + amortization gaps remain) |
| Batching across heterogeneous evidence | High — one batched forward pass for proposals; weighting reuses LW's vectorized math | Highest at query time — single forward pass per query, no sampling loop needed |
| Speed at B=1 vs LW | ≈ LW (same weighting cost) + one forward pass; per-query time falls as B grows | Faster than LW at query time; training cost amortized offline |
| Implementation complexity | Moderate; inference path ≈ LW (100s LOC) + offline trainer + recognition net (≈1000s LOC total) | Higher; new variational family + ELBO + gradient estimators + discrete relaxations (≈1000s LOC) |
| Mixed discrete/continuous | Native — proposal emits `nbn` mechanism parameters | Native for continuous (reparam); discrete needs relaxations |
| Main failure modes | Heavy-tailed weights / ESS collapse on rare or OOD evidence; mitigated by PSIS + `k̂` diagnostic | Mode-seeking, variance underestimation, amortization gap; discrete-gradient variance |
| Reuses "fit-once, query-many" infra | Yes (trained proposal artifact) | Yes (trained recognition net) |

**Which to build first.** **Engine A (the amortized-proposal self-normalized importance sampler).** Reasoning: (1) it preserves LW's correctness guarantee exactly — it is LW with a better proposal — so it is a safe drop-in that can only improve on LW and degrades gracefully to exact answers with more particles; (2) its inference-time code reuses `nbn`'s existing LW weighting machinery, so the new surface area is mostly the proposal network and an offline trainer that slot into the existing fit-once infrastructure; (3) it has the most direct published precedent on *discrete Bayesian networks specifically* (UM-IS), with concrete LW-comparison numbers; (4) it carries a built-in self-diagnostic (ESS / Pareto-`k̂`) so the engine can tell the user when it is unreliable and fall back to VE or more samples. Engine B is the natural second issue: it delivers the largest raw query-time speedup and a formal bound, but requires more new abstractions and gives up asymptotic exactness, so it is better as a deliberate second step once the proposal-network infrastructure from Engine A exists (the recognition network is largely reusable between them).

## Recommendations

1. **File Issue #1 (build first): "Amortized neural-proposal importance-sampling engine."** Prototype on `asia` and `sachs` (small discrete) first, validating that posteriors match VE within Monte Carlo error; then scale to `alarm`/`barley` and the n=100 synthetic discrete/continuous/hybrid families. Reuse the LW weighting kernel; add a conditional proposal network that emits per-node mechanism parameters. **Benchmark to beat:** at B=1024, per-query time should *decrease* relative to B=1 (genuine batching) and total time should beat LW at matched ESS. **Threshold to change course:** if Pareto-`k̂` routinely exceeds 0.7 on representative queries (indicating unreliable weights), invest in the tempered-SMC alternative instead of pushing the proposal harder.
2. **File Issue #2 (build second): "Amortized variational-inference engine"** with a structured (not pure mean-field) family and Gumbel-softmax/score-function gradients for discrete nodes. Reuse the recognition-network code from Issue #1. **Benchmark:** report the ELBO gap to VE's exact `log p(evidence)` on small networks as the bound-quality metric, and accuracy vs VE marginals on `alarm`. **Threshold:** if the amortization gap on held-out evidence patterns exceeds the approximation gap (per a Cremer-style decomposition), prefer a semi-amortized variant (a few per-query gradient steps initialized from the recognition net).
3. **Keep a tempered-SMC sampler (Neal 2001 / Del Moral et al. 2006) on the roadmap as the alternative asymptotic engine** if Engine A's proposal proves hard to train well on hybrid n=100 networks; it shares `nbn`'s `sample`/`log_prob` primitives and the parallel-resampling literature (Murray et al. 2016) makes it GPU-friendly, at the cost of weaker cross-query amortization.

## Caveats
- **Empirical comparisons are imperfectly matched to `nbn`'s exact setting.** The UM-IS numbers (≈2× ESS, 3–10× sample efficiency) come from large medical/synthetic discrete graphs at Babylon Health, not from bnlearn benchmarks per se; one sample-count statement in Walecki et al. 2018 appears to contain a typo (comparing 10⁵ vs 10⁵ where context implies 10⁵ vs 10⁶), so treat the "factor of ten or more" headline as indicative rather than precise. Inference compilation (Le et al. 2017) reports speedups qualitatively and via wall-clock times, not a clean SIS-vs-SIS factor.
- **Engine A's gains depend entirely on proposal quality.** If the learned proposal is poor (under-trained, or evidence is out-of-distribution relative to the training mask distribution), it reduces to — and can be marginally slower than — plain LW because of the extra forward pass. The ESS/`k̂` diagnostic is essential to detect this; without it the engine can silently return high-variance estimates.
- **Engine B's bound does not certify closeness to the true posterior in absolute terms** — the ELBO bounds `log p(evidence)` but the KL gap is generally unknown unless you also estimate `log p(evidence)` independently (e.g., via Engine A or VE on small networks). On multimodal posteriors the reverse-KL objective can confidently return a single mode.
- **Discrete latent variables are the shared technical risk.** Engine A handles them cleanly (the proposal emits categorical logits), but Engine B needs relaxed/score-function gradients whose variance can stall optimization on networks with many discrete nodes or near-deterministic CPDs (common in bnlearn's `alarm`/`barley`). Budget engineering time for this if Engine B is pursued.
- **I did not benchmark these engines inside `nbn` itself** — all speed/accuracy figures are from the cited literature on related systems. The recommended first step is the `asia`/`sachs` prototype precisely to confirm the batching and accuracy properties transfer to `nbn`'s implementation before committing to the larger build.

- **Engine B (amortized variational inference)** is the sequential second engine, reusing Engine A's recognition-network infrastructure. Tracked in issue **#182**; do not start until #181 merges.
